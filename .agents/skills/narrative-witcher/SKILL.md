# Writing a Witcher-grade open world

This is a reference, not a procedure: open the part you need. The order of work, the file format and
the criteria live in the story lane file (`.codex/agents/story.toml`); nothing here repeats them.

Everything below is craft observed from the people who made The Witcher 3 and from the folklore they
drew on. Where a claim comes from an interview or a talk, the reference file names who said it and
where, so a decision can be traced instead of believed.

## The five references

- `references/cdpr-quest-design.md` — how a quest is born, cut and judged: the definition of filler,
  the anatomy of the studio's best quest, choice and consequence, pacing, quest clusters, the rule
  that content dictates the size of the world. Open before structuring any quest.
- `references/living-world.md` — how a settlement is made to feel inhabited: points of interest
  instead of routes, schedules against the state of the world, ambient speech, environmental
  storytelling, region as one theme, districts as social layers, imperfection as a signal of life.
  Open before writing NPCs, schedules, barks or points of interest.
- `references/voice-and-folklore.md` — what makes speech read as this world rather than as generic
  fantasy: understatement and mockery as mechanism, exposition as a weapon, vocabulary as proof of
  knowledge, naming and phonetics per people, and how a monster is taken from real folklore. Open
  before writing any line, name or creature.
- `references/lore-tir-na-lia.md` — the elven world, its city, its people, the Wild Hunt after the
  war, the White Frost, unicorns, Elder Blood and Ciri, every entry marked as book canon, game canon,
  announced, rumour, or ours. Open before writing lore, and whenever a fact about the world is about
  to be stated as true.
- `references/roblox-narrative.md` — what the platform does to narrative: how players actually meet
  story here, the four channels that carry it, skipping, one speaking character, journals, bubbles,
  multiplayer visibility. Open before deciding how a piece of story reaches the player.
- `references/judge-checklist.md` — for a fresh judge only. It lists the forms that mark writing as
  machine-made and the extraction checks that expose a fake voice. Reading it while writing plants
  the very forms it names: name the path when briefing a judge, and do not open it yourself.

## What this craft is, in short

**A quest is a story, not a task.** It has a beginning, a middle and an end, and something in the
player's picture of the world is different at the end. The failure mode has a name in this studio and
at CDPR: filler — a step that asks the player to fetch, carry or clear without a word being said, a
choice being made or anything changing. Bringing an object is legitimate when the bringing opens a
conversation or forces a decision.

**The world is a system, not a backdrop.** Places have an economy, an owner, a thing that is broken
here and not next door, and a history that is legible in objects before anyone speaks. People live
inside that system and it presses on them whether or not the player arrives.

**People are built from circumstances upward.** What someone does with their hands, what they owe and
what they can lose shapes behaviour more than any adjective about their character. Character shows in
the order of what they give up first and in what makes them break their own rule.

**Choice is only choice when both sides cost.** No option is marked correct, both are believable for
the people involved, and what follows is explained by how the world works rather than by the author's
morality. Consequences are visible in the world, and some of them arrive later than expected.

**The environment speaks first, text second, a talking character third.** This is true of the Witcher
and doubly true on Roblox, where most players are moving while the text is on screen.

**Humour is the counterweight, not the decoration.** What is funny is a recognisable truth about a
person, carried by behaviour under pain. Comic lines that comment on the situation instead of acting
in it read as the writer talking.

**Folklore is the source, not the invention.** Monsters and rites come from real Slavic, Celtic and
Norse material, keep their name, and have one mechanism changed on purpose. A fact invented about the
established world is worse than a fact left out.

## Two failure modes that swallow this kind of writing

**Generic fantasy.** The test: take the rules, names and creatures of the place and move them into
another fantasy game. If nothing has to change, a genre was described, not a world. What resists the
move is what is specific: a broken local thing, a trade only here, a word for an ordinary object that
outsiders get wrong, a rite that costs somebody something.

**The lore bible nobody plays.** Rules the player never meets are weight without payoff. A rule earns
its place by explaining somebody's choice on screen, and a rule that a quest's turn depends on is
visible in the world before it works. When a hole appears, the cheap answer is a new rule; the answer
that pays is a person with a motive.
