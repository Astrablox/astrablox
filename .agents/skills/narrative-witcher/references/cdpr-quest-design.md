# Quest design as CD Projekt Red practises it

Sources are named inline so a decision can be traced. Quotes are from designers about their craft;
nothing here is an example of game text to imitate.

## What filler is, exactly

Mateusz Tomaszkiewicz, quest lead: "When I think of 'fetch quest' there's a picture of a shopping
list in my mind – get me 10 bear pelts, 5 eggs, 6 candles… quests that don't engage player at all and
are there just to add some extra hours of gameplay." The second wall of the same rule, from the same
answer: "I don't mind at all, if I have to bring an object to an NPC, if the fact of bringing that
item triggers an interesting conversation" (CDPR forum Q&A, 2016, vulkk.com mirror).

Karolina Stachyra: "[Every quest] has to have some sort of impact on the player… We said 'no' to any
sort of fetch quests, as they don't provide any substance to the game – they're only there to fill
the space" (Game Informer, 2016).

Philipp Weber, narrative director of The Witcher 4, on what a new designer is told first: "we don't
do fetch quests"; the rule still holds on the next game, and each designer wrote "10 times as many
ideas as those that landed in the game" (GamesRadar, 2025).

They broke their own rule and named it: "there were a few quests – even though we had a rule against
it – that felt padded; like fetch quests… because we stuck with ideas we shouldn't have." The lesson
carried into Hearts of Stone: "Nothing was added simply to make something else make sense"
(Tomaszkiewicz, Stevivor, 2015).

Patrick Mills: "All of our quests need to tell a story, and the story has to be interesting. Every
monster hunt in the Witcher 3 has its own arc: it has a beginning, a middle, and an end" (PCGamesN,
2016). Kuba Szamałek on the reason: "We didn't want the player to think that we don't respect his or
her time" (Kotaku, 2015).

## How a quest is born and how it is cut

- It starts as one or two sentences, and most pitches die there: "designers list their ideas in one
  to two sentence pitches, then we pick the ones that have potential" (Tomaszkiewicz).
- Editing rule: "think about the element that makes your quest interesting, focus on highlighting
  that element and remove everything around it" (Tomaszkiewicz). Cutting is the main move, not adding.
- Growth goes either way: from a baseline statement upward (Stachyra's example of asking what a
  horse would say if it could talk) or from a theme downward (a fairytale land gone wrong).
- Mills looks for "the core themes, the ideas that could support some sort of emotional, mental
  investment"; the seed can be a scene, a joke, a situation, or a piece of gameplay.
- Clichés: Mateusz Sasko (Digital Dragons 2017) crosses out the first ideas of a brainstorm "because
  those are the clichés". Two legitimate uses: consciously, in the open; or not at all.
- A character either carries a whole story or is deleted: "some characters are so important that they
  need whole stories to be told about them, otherwise it's better to remove them completely" (Sasko,
  MCV 2016).
- A writer's idea is translated into the language of play, not staged as cutscene: quest designers
  "talk extensively with the writers to understand the core idea… so it can be adjusted in a way that
  won't kill that original idea but will make the quest work better in terms of game mechanics"
  (Tomaszkiewicz, MCV).
- Open world means the player arrives wrong: "from the very beginning we have to think about all the
  possible paths and unusual ways to finish the quest… our approach was always focused around
  supporting more paths than blocking them" (Sasko, MCV).

## Choice, consequence and moral greyness

- "We tried to craft choices that are hard to make, but believable for this character"; the studio
  argues against false binaries of the save-the-orphans kind, and states its first principle: "Our
  main design principle is immersion. You should feel you are impacting people's lives and their
  world" (Tomaszkiewicz, startvideojuegos).
- "We also tried to avoid the black and white division of choices… you are in a tough situation and
  you have to manage somehow… Sometimes you will even feel that, 'Okay, none of these solutions are
  good'" (GameSpot, 2015). In the same interview, the consequence that follows a choice is justified
  by how that society works — a wartime village treats arson as sabotage and hangs for it — not by
  the writer's judgement of the player.
- Mills: "In life you don't get those easy decisions with immediate repercussions. You make a choice
  and you have to live with it… We remind people that there aren't any easy answers"; and on the
  audience: "We trust our audience… We know that you aren't stupid."
- Weber: "We ask difficult questions, but don't preach easy answers"; a monstrous character can still
  be met with sympathy without the game endorsing him.
- Stachyra: the protagonist "is never a side until the player chooses he becomes one. We never impose
  on you"; and the world is brutal because its source material is, but the point of the darkness is
  what shows through it.
- Side content changes the main line and does not rewind: ignore a side thread and the outcome
  arrives later inside the main story, with no way back (Tomaszkiewicz, GameSpot). Finished contracts
  change how a settlement treats the player.

## Ten lessons Sasko took to GDC 2023

Titles are his; the gloss is what each means when writing.
1. **Create the desire for story** — withhold a key fact on purpose so the player wants it.
2. **Control the emotion** — "You need to control what the players are thinking and feeling", rather
   than assembling cool sequences without soul.
3. **Let the story breathe** — each thread gets its own screen time and space.
4. **Human moments** — small cheap gestures, read from body language, build the bond.
5. **Cut repeated exposition** — "Exposition can also be partially moved into the world."
6. **Signal over noise** — what matters is delivered in focused, isolated moments, never while the
   player is fighting or steering.
7. **Impact** — "For choices to have meaning, they have to have impact"; dilemmas stay ambiguous.
8. **Delayed consequences** — an early choice returns at an unexpected moment, and the return is
   visible.
9. **Against overdesign** — "A game is not a life simulation, it is entertainment."
10. **Novelty anchored in the familiar** — the most advanced yet acceptable thing, not the strangest.

## The anatomy of their best quest

Family Matters / the Bloody Baron, as its authors describe it (PC Gamer; Destructoid on Sasko's talk;
GamesRadar 2025).

- The pitch was one sentence: a lord asks the protagonist to kill a monster and offers, in exchange,
  information he needs.
- The character personifies the region: a soldier back from war with drink and damage, unstable and
  unpleasant, and holding real virtues at the same time. "We needed a character that personified
  Velen, with all of its beauty and troubles."
- The region has one topic and the quest contains all of it: "Every area in The Witcher 3 touches a
  different topic and has a different atmosphere… In this place there's war, famine, death, missing
  people, old rituals, and terrifying monsters. And Family Matters was crafted to contain all of
  these elements."
- The antagonist mirrors the hero — two fathers who lost their families, two men with blood on their
  hands — so judging him means judging the player character.
- Weight comes from doubt, not from balance sheets: "There are very few people in our lives, even if
  we love them, that we've never felt conflicted about. That's what makes a character real… You
  hesitate when presented with a choice." Sympathy was engineered early and deliberately, so that the
  later revelation costs something.
- The room tells the story before anyone does: traces of violence and drink read from the objects in
  his hall.
- The monster is folklore carrying the theme: a stillborn child improperly buried becomes the
  creature, so the quest's subject becomes a physical opponent, and the resolution is a rite rather
  than a fight.
- Pacing was drawn as a graph of tension against time, with scenes colour-coded by kind (talk, fight,
  travel-with-talk): "The most important parts in the story are when the tension is the lowest and
  when the tension is the highest." Too much uninterrupted gameplay and "you start to lose purpose…
  There's nobody telling you 'Yeah, actually, you're doing it because there's a purpose'."
- The player's arc through it: "I get engaged, and then I get involved, and then I start to care";
  the whole build-up serves one release near the end.
- Endings differ in kind, not in quality: depending on the choice it is a story of redemption or a
  bleak tragedy. Stachyra: "There's only your ending."
- Internal check: "designers in the quest team will play each others' quests and give feedback… We
  just rework all the time."

## The world is sized by content, not the reverse

- Miles Tost: "the quest content dictated the size of the world, not the other way around… This
  probably helped us to not end up with a world that feels artificially bloated" (Kotaku).
- Procedural contracts were built and thrown away: the gap between handmade and generated content was
  too visible to ship. "Everything that is made in a level was hand-crafted."
- Szamałek held a hard cap on dialogue lines for a world quest and had to fight for every exception.
  Do not carry the number; carry the principle: a world quest lives in short speech and in the place
  itself, and a long scene is an exception that has to be earned by the player stopping on their own.
- Side content is grouped: "We have placed them in what we call 'quest clusters'" around hubs, rather
  than sprinkled across the map (Tomaszkiewicz).
- Their own admitted failure is density without stories: on one region, they "tried to add more
  question marks but couldn't" — the map filled with points that had nothing behind them (Sasko,
  GamesRadar 2026).

## What The Witcher 4 says about the direction

The announced side-content shape: a frightened community has turned a monster into a social system;
the creature is dangerous, and the ritual around it also licenses the community's own cruelty. The
player investigates a local horror, makes a choice, kills the visible threat, and then faces
consequences a sword cannot settle (CDPR material on the Stromford teaser, 2025-2026).
