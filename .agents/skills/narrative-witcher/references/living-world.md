# The living world: how a place is made inhabited

## NPCs live off points of interest, not routes

Philipp Weber on how the crowd of The Witcher 3 works: "We don't give them specific paths. We give
them spots of interest that are available and then they're the ones doing the pathfinding… for the 50
sailors at Novigrad harbour, for example, we made 200 possible spots, so even if all 50 were active
at the same time, they would have options" (PC Gamer, 2026).

What this means for writing a schedule: describe where a person can be and what they are doing with
their hands there, at which part of the day and in which state of the world. Do not choreograph a
path. A place that is worth standing in is a place where somebody has work: a bench, a net, a stall, a
grave, a fire, a doorway they are watching. The number of such places has to exceed the number of
people, or the settlement locks up and the illusion goes with it.

Reactions to the player need exceptions built in. When they made everyone drop what they carried and
run from a fight in town, the result was absurd — people dropped things nobody would ever drop. Decide
who does not run, who runs but comes back, who was going to leave anyway.

Players test the simulation deliberately: "If a village only has eight houses, people will want to see
if the people truly exist in the world or if it's just fakery." A small settlement is examined more
closely than a city, not less.

## What holds up in a day of that world, and what breaks

From a day spent watching one village (Kotaku, "A Day in the Life of a Witcher 3 Village"):

- The village wakes and sleeps at the same hours, and that reads as a rhythm rather than as a
  schedule, because the waking is spent on visible work.
- Weather rewrites behaviour and speech: in rain the ones who keep working speak in single sentences,
  mostly complaining about the rain; in sun there is laundry, mending, sharpening, and gossip.
- A rumour travels: one person carries a single topic around the settlement and each neighbour
  answers it in their own way. This is a cheap, strong device — one subject, many refusals.
- Night belongs to somebody else than day: whoever comes out after dark is a different population.
- What breaks the illusion, named by the writer: the same bark on every pass, children who never
  sleep, nobody ever eating. The failure is repetition without state, not simplicity.
- The article's conclusion is the important one: the humanity of that game came from the writing of
  small characters and their morally knotted problems, not from the simulation. The simulation buys
  the ground the writing stands on.

CDPR's own comparison after their next game: the gap between hand-authored characters and systemic
crowd characters reads "like a canyon". Whatever the systems do, the characters a player talks to are
written by hand.

## Ambient speech

Barks are the character of the region spoken aloud. What makes them work:

- Each is about what the speaker is doing or suffering right now: the work in their hands, the
  weather on them, their body, their money, their debts, their distrust of the stranger.
- None of them explains the world. A villager does not narrate the history of the region; a villager
  complains, boasts, haggles, warns, jokes, and gets something wrong.
- They change with the state of the world: day and night, rain and sun, before and after a fight,
  before and after the player's choice landed. The same person after the player's choice does not
  greet them the same way.
- The greeting a settlement gives a stranger is a compressed portrait of the settlement. It is worth
  writing that one line as carefully as a quest line.
- Two people talking past each other in earshot carries more than one person announcing something.

## Environmental storytelling

- A region is one theme plus the first thing the player sees. For their war-ravaged region the theme
  was desperation, hunger and hopelessness, and the first thing on the road was a hanging tree:
  "which, I think, sells the idea pretty well" (CDPR, Vice, 2016).
- Stachyra on the method: bodies left by the roadside, sweets scattered around a bog to lure children
  toward what lives there. "Symbolism and playing with connotations are very important for us and we
  frequently use them as tools to evoke emotions."
- The world is built from the story: "we use the story as a base to determine the basic dimension and
  tone of each area" (Michał Janiszewski, 80.lv). Level design starts from quest design: "Everything
  starts with quest design. It's like a foundation for us that sets up the frames we start to work
  with" (CDPR AnsweRED podcast, 2026).
- Districts are social layers: each district has a backstory and a different feel; dirt and crowding
  at the outer rim, richer and cleaner toward the centre; in another city, poor at the bottom of the
  hill and rich at the top. Height and distance carry class without a word.
- Imperfection is the signal of life: "By not using a grid for mesh placement and having everything
  slightly imperfect we make it more believable, like a living person built it, not a machine"
  (80.lv). Objects stand where hands left them.
- Reference, never copy: the regions are based on real Slavic, Celtic and Scandinavian cultures and
  landscapes, and no real place is reproduced one to one.
- Landmarks and rhythm: a tall thing visible from everywhere orients the player; a narrow enclosed
  passage that opens into a large space converts pressure into relief (level design analysis of
  Oxenfurt, Game Developer, 2020). Signposts and other in-world markers do the work a HUD would
  otherwise do.
- Even what players never consciously notice buys belief — a whole meeting went on the material of a
  city wall: "It's not something players notice on a conscious level… I think it all comes together
  to create this illusion of a real place" (Szamałek).
- Points of interest without a story behind them are the studio's own admitted mistake. Types that
  earned their place: a settlement held by bandits that repopulates with traders once cleared; a
  guarded hoard; prisoners; a monster nest; smuggling. Each has a visible consequence in the world
  after the player deals with it.
- Economy of a small studio, stated by CDPR themselves: many of the stories scattered through the
  open world were conceived by looking at assets that already existed and asking what could have
  happened there. Build stories on what the world lane has already made whenever the story does not
  suffer for it.
