# Voice, naming and folklore

No sample lines appear in this file on purpose. A sample line becomes a template: the next twenty
lines get written to resemble it. What follows are the mechanisms that produce the voice.

## What makes speech read as this world

- **Understatement and mockery, not banter.** The protagonist's manner in the source books and games
  is dry humour and withheld emotion. In the novels the author's habit is to introduce a hostile line
  with a taunt or a sneer rather than with an insult: the characteristic move is needling, not
  swearing (study of Sapkowski's idiolect, University of Białystok). Whoever mocks in a scene is
  telling you where they think they stand.
- **Slavic storytelling avoids clear moral binaries** — the localisation team names this as the thing
  they had to preserve when the game left Polish, together with sarcasm, cynicism and regional
  speech. Idioms were rebuilt to keep irony, regional attitude or sarcasm rather than translated
  word for word; signs, notices, item descriptions and place names kept their regional flavour;
  folklore terms were left untranslated and explained through context instead (VEQTA case study,
  2025). Two rules follow: an idiom in this world is remade, not borrowed from ours; and a folk term
  is used and made clear by the situation around it, never glossed.
- **Speech carries the action.** In the novels, pages run on dialogue with almost no description, and
  the reader always knows what is happening around the conversation; the connective prose between
  lines is simple, effective, even choppy. Write the scene so what happens is legible from what people
  say and do to each other.
- **Every line does something to the person it is aimed at.** A line is not ready until the verb is
  named: what is this person doing to the other with these words — pushing, testing, stalling,
  refusing, buying time, changing the subject. Lines that state feelings or facts to each other are
  the writer talking through two mouths.
- **Exposition is a weapon in somebody's hand.** Nobody tells anybody what they both already know. A
  fact reaches the player because someone needs to use it: to threaten, to sell, to excuse, to warn.
  Whatever can be seen instead of said is seen.
- **Subtext is not fog.** The line is understood the first time at the level of what is said; the
  second meaning sits under a clear surface. A line the player has to decode twice is a failed line,
  not a deep one.
- **Under pressure, speech gets simpler and dirtier, not shorter and cleaner.** Polished clipped
  fragments under maximum pressure read as control where there can be none. A short line is legitimate
  as a tactic; a long one is legitimate when the tension has earned it. Length is never a character
  trait: the moment length becomes someone's signature, every character starts sounding the same.
- **Vocabulary proves knowledge.** A person names the things of their trade with the words of that
  trade, and names an ordinary object the way locals name it. The word someone uses for a common thing
  is what marks them as belonging here or not.
- **A stylised world licenses more figurative speech than a modern one**, inside the rules the world
  set for itself. One person in the cast may be the one who out-talks everybody; only one.
- **Humour is a truth about a person plus the pain of living it.** With no pain in the moment, it is a
  quip. Comedy is placed as behaviour — what someone does while saying it — not as commentary on the
  situation.
- **The extras have their own trade and their own words.** A background speaker who exists only to
  react is still someone with work in their hands.
- **Reference to the wider canon is allusion, never recap.** A famous thing is mentioned by someone
  who has a reason to mention it, in one line, and the game moves on. The player who does not know
  it loses nothing; the player who does gets a jolt. The studio did exactly this with the
  protagonist's past in the game that introduced her.
- **Respect for the player is the house tone**: "We respect the player's intelligence. We treat
  videogames as a medium for adults" (Sasko, quoting lead writer Marcin Blacha). Nothing is explained
  twice, and nothing is softened because a player might miss it.

## Names

- Each people has its own phonetics and they do not mix. The elven tongue of this canon is Celtic in
  sound; human place names in the source games come from Slavic and Polish roots; the northern isles
  are Scandinavian. Read the lore reference before coining a name, and coin it inside one of those
  systems.
- A place name carries what happened there. The strongest names in the source game are plain
  descriptions of an event or a feature that a local would use.
- The name a people uses for outsiders is a social map in one word. Who calls the player what, and
  who refuses to call them anything, says more about the region than a paragraph of lore.

## Folklore as the source of monsters and rites

- The method, from the studio: the author of the books drew on so many mythologies "it would be
  easier to list the mythologies he didn't draw from", and the studio dug into their own Eastern and
  Northern European material rather than inventing bestiary from scratch.
- Take a creature or a rite that exists in real Slavic, Celtic or Norse folklore; keep its name and
  its origin story; change one mechanism deliberately, and let that change be the thing the quest is
  about. The creature born of an improperly buried stillbirth, laid to rest by a burial rite rather
  than killed, is the model case: the theme of the quest and the body of the monster are the same
  thing.
- Folk creatures in the original material are usually parties to a bargain, not obstacles. A forest
  spirit that shepherds bought protection from with bread, salt and eggs at a crossroads punished
  greed, not existence. Turning such a being into a boss with a binary fight throws away the
  interesting half; keep the bargain available.
- What was lost when the source game simplified a spirit into an insect is instructive: the more
  specific folk version was more interesting than the redesign.
- Rites of remembrance and mourning that really exist carry a quest better than invented ceremonies,
  because they come with rules, seasons and people who take them seriously and people who do not.
- The genre's own myths are fair game for irony: the author himself takes the standard legend of a
  creature and has a character debunk it in the same breath. Myth plus a local's contempt for the
  myth is the house tone in one move.

## The line between our invention and the canon

Anything stated about the established world must be traceable to the lore reference and marked there
as book canon, game canon, announced or rumour. Anything we invent is ours, marked as ours in the
lore bible, and consistent with what the canon has already fixed. An invented fact presented as canon
is worse than no fact: it will contradict a player who knows the material, and it will contradict us
in the next scene.
